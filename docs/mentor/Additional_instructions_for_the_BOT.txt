Using the Adult Chair method to help people identify and regulate emotional responses by understanding three distinct internal "voices" or parts of ourselves. Here's a detailed breakdown of the concepts and steps:
Core Concept: The Three Chairs
The Child Chair ( Pained persona)represents our emotional, reactive self - where we experience hurt, fear, excitement, creativity, and raw emotions. This part holds our wounds, triggers, and unmet needs from the past.
The Adolescent Chair( Protective manager) embodies our rebellious, defensive, or controlling aspects. This part often reacts with anger, defiance, people-pleasing, or attempts to manage and control situations when we feel threatened.
The Adult Chair ( The individuated self) is our wise, grounded, authentic self that can observe situations clearly, make conscious choices, and respond rather than react. This is where emotional regulation and healthy decision-making happen.
Steps to Practice the Adult Chair Method
Step 1: Pause and Identify When you notice emotional dysregulation (intense reactions, feeling triggered, or behaving in ways that don't serve you), pause and ask: "Which chair am I in right now?"
Step 2: Acknowledge Without Judgment Recognize which part of you is activated without criticism. You might notice Child Chair feelings of hurt or fear, or Adolescent Chair reactions like defensiveness or control.
Step 3: Validate the Emotion Honor what the Child or Adolescent part is experiencing. These reactions often stem from legitimate needs or past wounds that deserve acknowledgment.
Step 4: Move to the Adult Chair Consciously shift into your Adult Chair by:
Taking deep breaths
Grounding yourself in the present moment
Asking "What do I actually need right now?"
Considering your values and long-term goals
Step 5: Respond from the Adult Make choices and take actions from this centered place rather than reacting from emotional dysregulation.
Finding Underlying Emotional Dysregulation
The method helps uncover dysregulation by teaching you to recognize patterns in your emotional responses. When you consistently react from Child or Adolescent chairs in certain situations, this points to underlying triggers, unmet needs, or unhealed wounds that require attention and care.
The goal isn't to eliminate the Child or Adolescent parts, but to have the Adult Chair be the primary decision-maker while honouring all parts of yourself.

TAME THE MIND - NAME YOUR FEELINGS

Step 1: Understand the Basics
Familiarize with Key Components: 
Observation: What you see or hear without judgment.
Feelings: How you feel in response to what you observe.
Needs: The underlying needs or values that cause those feelings.
Requests: Concrete actions you would like to take to meet your needs.
Step 2: Practice Observation
Identify Observations vs. Evaluations: Practice distinguishing between objective observations and subjective evaluations. For example, instead of saying “You’re late,” try “I noticed you arrived 15 minutes after the agreed time.”
Practicing observation versus evaluation is a key aspect of Communicating with the heart rather than the head. Here are some examples to help clarify the difference:
### Example 1: On Punctuality
- **Observation**: “I noticed you arrived at 10:15 AM, while we agreed to meet at 10:00 AM.”
- **Evaluation**: “You are always late and don’t care about our time.”
### Example 2: On Work Performance
- **Observation**: “You submitted the report with only three sections completed.”
- **Evaluation**: “You didn’t do your job properly.”
### Example 3: On Personal Relationships
- **Observation**: “I saw that you spent the evening on your phone instead of talking with me.”
- **Evaluation**: “You’re ignoring me and don’t value our time together.”
### Example 4: On Behavior
- **Observation**: “I noticed you raised your voice during our conversation.”
- **Evaluation**: “You’re being aggressive and rude.”
### Example 5: On Team Dynamics
- **Observation**: “I observed that during the meeting, you interrupted several team members.”
- **Evaluation**: “You’re not being respectful to others.”
Practice Tips:
- **Identify the Trigger**: When you feel triggered, pause and ask yourself how you can describe the situation without adding judgment.
- **Use “I” Statements**: Frame your observations from your perspective to reduce defensiveness (e.g., “I noticed…” rather than “You did…”).
Practicing these distinctions can help you communicate more effectively and create a more understanding environment in your interactions.
Step 3: Explore Feelings
Use Feeling Lists: Create a list of feelings (Box 1 & 2 below) (e.g., happy, frustrated, sad) and practice identifying your feelings in various situations.
Journaling: Write about your daily experiences, focusing on how they made you feel.
Step 4: Identify Needs
Needs Inventory: Familiarise yourself with a list of universal human needs (Box 3 below) (e.g., safety, love, connection). Reflect on how your feelings are connected to these needs.
Reflect on Your Needs: In difficult situations, take time to identify what needs are not being met.
Step 5: Make Requests
Practice Formulating Requests: Focus on what you want rather than what you don’t want. For example, instead of saying “Stop interrupting me,” try “I would appreciate it if you could let me finish my thoughts.”
Use Positive Language: Frame your requests positively and ensure they are specific and actionable.
Formulating requests is about making clear, specific, and positive requests that can help meet your needs. Here are some examples to illustrate this:
### Example 1: Requesting Help
- **Instead of**: “You never help with the housework.”
- **Request**: “Would you be willing to help me with the dishes tonight?”
### Example 2: Seeking Attention
- **Instead of**: “You’re always on your phone when I’m talking to you.”
- **Request**: “Could you put your phone down and give me your full attention while we talk?”
### Example 3: Setting Boundaries
- **Instead of**: “Stop interrupting me.”
- **Request**: “Can we agree that we each take turns speaking during our meetings?”
### Example 4: Asking for Feedback
- **Instead of**: “You never tell me how I’m doing at work.”
- **Request**: “Could you give me some feedback on my project by the end of the week?”
### Example 5: Changing a Behaviour
- **Instead of**: “You need to be more responsible with your schedule.”
- **Request**: “Would you be able to send me a reminder a day before our meetings?”
### Example 6: Scheduling Time Together
- **Instead of**: “You never want to spend time with me.”
- **Request**: “Would you be willing to set aside some time this weekend for a movie together?”
Key Tips for Formulating Requests (SPLACE):
1. **Be Specific**: Clearly state what you want the person to do.
2. **Use Positive Language**: Focus on what you want rather than what you don’t want.
3. **Make it Actionable**: Ensure your request is something the other person can realistically do.
4. **Invite Collaboration**: Consider asking for their perspective, like “How do you feel about this?”
5. Express Gratitude: Consider adding a note of appreciation, like “I’d really appreciate your help with this.”
Practicing these requests can help foster more open and effective communication, increasing the likelihood of positive outcomes.
